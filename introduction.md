# Introduction

Humans are remarkably quick at understanding visual scenes. A brief glance is often enough to identify where we are, what is happening, and what to do next (Oliva & Torralba, 2007). This speed is not simply the result of the eye taking in shapes and colours; it depends heavily on what the observer already knows about the kind of environment they are looking at (Bar, 2004). Predictive coding frameworks account for this by proposing that the brain constantly generates expectations about what it is about to see and compares those expectations to the incoming image (Friston, 2010; Rao & Ballard, 1999). When the image matches what the brain expected, processing is fast and effortless. When it does not, the mismatch registers as a prediction error that the brain must resolve (Clark, 2013). Everyday visual experience, from recognising a café to navigating a crowded street, therefore rests on a foundation of learned regularities that make the familiar feel fluent and the unfamiliar feel effortful.

A growing body of work shows that when these expectations are violated, the cost is measurable. Greene et al. (2015) showed observers real-world scenes that depicted either probable or improbable situations, carefully matched on colour, spatial frequency, and edge density. Participants viewing improbable scenes produced poorer verbal descriptions with more errors, required longer viewing times to recognise that anything was unusual, and were less able to detect the images when embedded in visual noise. Because the images were matched on low-level features, these deficits could not be explained by the stimuli themselves and instead reflected difficulty at the level of expectation. McLean et al. (2023) extended this picture by showing that expectations generated from preceding context facilitated scene categorisation when targets were congruous and actively interfered with it when targets were incongruous, with electrophysiological markers revealing effects across early perceptual, semantic, and structural stages. Ruzzoli et al. (2020) similarly found that surrealistic images containing object-scene incongruities triggered automatic conflict-detection signals, even when observers were not asked to attend to the oddity. Together, these findings support a consistent conclusion: the visual system is tuned to the regularities of everyday environments, and encountering elements that violate those regularities disrupts processing in ways that can be measured both behaviourally and neurally.

What this literature has largely overlooked, however, is whether these expectation violations also change how a person feels. Studies in the predictive-coding tradition have focused on perceptual accuracy and processing time, treating violated expectations as a purely cognitive problem. Barrett and Bar (2009) challenged this framing by arguing that affective responses are generated alongside perceptual predictions rather than after them. On their account, the brain's predictions about incoming input are inherently valenced, such that stimuli that are difficult to reconcile with expectations are accompanied by negative feeling states before any deliberate evaluation takes place. Supporting this view, Sadeghi et al. (2024) showed that affective valence can be decoded directly from the visual features of scenes, and Caplette et al. (2020) found that expectation-driven activity in object-processing regions is modulated by the affective value of the anticipated objects. Together, these findings suggest that perceptual and affective prediction are intertwined rather than sequential processes.


Schreuder et al. (2016) offer a useful framework for thinking about this perceptual-affective link. Although the framework has been influential in multisensory environmental research, it has primarily been developed and tested in contexts involving combinations of sensory modalities such as audio-visual or audio-olfactory pairings, and its applicability to the unisensory visual case (particularly where visual incongruence is manipulated systematically) remains untested. They propose that responses to environmental stimuli involve two related but distinct processes. External assessment is how the observer reads the environment itself (whether it looks expected, coherent, or orderly). Internal assessment is the emotional response the environment produces in the observer (whether it feels comfortable, uneasy, or pleasant). The two processes are coupled but not identical: the observer first registers what the environment is like, and then, either in parallel or in quick succession, responds to it emotionally in a way that is partly shaped by that initial reading. In their review, Schreuder et al. (2016) found that incongruent sensory pairings, for example mismatched audio-visual or audio-olfactory combinations, tended to produce negative emotional appraisals. This is consistent with the idea that when the environment is harder to make sense of externally, the internal response shifts in a negative direction. Applied to visual scenes, the framework makes a specific prediction: a scene containing out-of-place elements, which is by definition harder to reconcile with expectations, should also be experienced as less comfortable.

The idea that subtle visual atypicality can rapidly shape affective responses is further supported by research on social perception. Sasson et al. (2017) examined how neurotypical observers evaluated autistic individuals across several thin-slice paradigms, including a condition using only static photographs. Neurotypical participants formed markedly less favourable first impressions of autistic targets across all stimulus types, rating them as less approachable and less likeable, and reporting reduced willingness to interact. These judgments formed within seconds and persisted even when audio and conversational content was removed, isolating visual presentation as sufficient to drive the effect. Importantly, the pattern held regardless of what the autistic targets had actually said, indicating that subtle differences in visual appearance and expression, rather than the substance of communication, were doing the work. Visual atypicality can therefore produce discomfort and social avoidance before any meaningful interaction takes place, which suggests that the affective cost of violated expectations extends from scene perception into how people respond to one another.

Despite this convergence across scene perception, multisensory affect, and social cognition, no study has directly tested whether scene-level oddities of the kind used by Greene et al. (2015) produce measurable changes in self-reported comfort, nor whether the perceived unexpectedness of a scene (reflecting external assessment) predicts the comfort response (reflecting internal assessment). The present study addresses this gap by investigating the effect of visual oddity on perceived comfort in everyday scenes and by testing whether perceived unexpectedness predicts the comfort response.

## Aims and Hypotheses

The aim of this study is to investigate whether visual scenes containing contextually incongruent elements produce lower self-reported comfort than scenes depicting expected situations, and whether perceived unexpectedness predicts the comfort response. Testing this relationship provides a direct empirical evaluation of Schreuder et al.'s (2016) external-internal assessment framework within the visual-scene domain. The activity level of each scene, defined as the number of people and the degree of human activity present, is included as an additional factor in the design to control for the possibility that crowding independently influences comfort, so that any observed differences in comfort can be attributed to the presence of oddity rather than to the density of activity in the scene.

Three hypotheses are proposed. First, a significant main effect of oddity on comfort is expected: participants will report significantly lower comfort for scenes containing out-of-place elements than for scenes depicting expected situations, as measured on a five-point comfort scale. This prediction follows from Greene et al.'s (2015) finding that improbable scenes disrupt processing, together with Barrett and Bar's (2009) argument that processing difficulty generates negative affect. Second, a non-significant main effect of activity level on comfort and a non-significant activity × oddity interaction are expected. This pattern would support the intended role of activity level as a control factor and indicate that the effect of oddity on comfort does not depend on how busy the scene is. Third, oddity scenes are expected to be rated as significantly more unexpected than normal scenes on a five-point unexpectedness scale, and unexpectedness ratings are expected to significantly predict comfort ratings, with greater perceived unexpectedness associated with lower comfort. This final prediction operationalises Schreuder et al.'s (2016) proposed link between external and internal assessment.

## Significance of the Study

The proposed study offers several contributions that together advance both the theoretical understanding of how expectation violations shape affective response and the practical question of how that understanding might inform the treatment of difference in everyday environments.

Theoretically, the study extends predictive coding accounts of scene perception (Friston, 2010; Rao & Ballard, 1999) beyond the perceptual and cognitive domains in which they have primarily been tested. If oddity scenes reliably reduce comfort, and if unexpectedness predicts comfort, this would offer direct empirical support for Barrett and Bar's (2009) proposal that affective and perceptual predictions are generated in concert rather than sequentially. It would also provide a test of Schreuder et al.'s (2016) external-internal assessment framework using visual-scene stimuli with systematically manipulated contextual congruence, clarifying whether the two-pathway model generalises from the multisensory environments in which it was developed to the unisensory visual case. Importantly, establishing this mechanism in non-social scenes speaks directly to how the same mechanism is likely to operate in social perception. Sasson et al. (2017) found that neurotypical observers rapidly form less favourable impressions of autistic targets based on visual presentation alone, but their paradigm did not isolate the affective machinery driving those judgments. If the present study shows that contextually incongruent elements in non-social scenes produce reduced comfort through perceived unexpectedness, this offers a shared affective-prediction account that spans both the non-social and social domains, rather than treating the rapid negative response to autistic presentation as a phenomenon specific to social cognition.

Methodologically, the study introduces a scene-family design in which multiple variants of each everyday setting are constructed to orthogonally manipulate oddity and activity level, allowing the contribution of contextual incongruence to affective response to be isolated while the underlying environment is held constant. A feature of this design worth highlighting is that oddity is treated as a property of the relationship between an element and its context, rather than as a property of the element itself. The same element that appears incongruous in one scene would not appear so in another where it is expected. This framing carries a conceptual implication that extends beyond the immediate study: whether a feature of a person or environment reads as "out of place" depends on the expectations the observer brings, not on any inherent property of the feature. Applied to autistic traits, which are stable characteristics of how a person moves, speaks, or engages, this framing recasts the apparent “oddity” of such traits as a function of the normative expectations of the surrounding context rather than of the traits themselves.

Practically, the findings would carry implications for the design of environments in which contextual coherence shapes how people feel: from retail and hospitality spaces to workplaces, community settings, and other everyday environments. The direction that most warrants further investigation concerns how neurodivergent people are perceived in these environments, particularly in the workplace. Approaches to autism differ on whether intervention should aim to change the autistic person or the environment, with some traditions emphasising behavioural modification of autistic traits (e.g., Lovaas, 1987) and others arguing that such traits are stable characteristics and that the burden of adjustment should fall on the surrounding context (e.g., Milton, 2012). The present study's framing aligns with the latter position. If perceived unexpectedness proves to drive reduced comfort in non-social scenes, this would raise the question of whether a similar mechanism contributes to the rapid negative evaluations that Sasson et al. (2017) documented in response to autistic presentation, and whether interventions that shift observer expectations (rather than the autistic person) could be effective levers for reducing that response. Workplace practices such as neurodivergent inclusion training, sustained exposure to autistic colleagues across roles and seniority, and the normalisation of autistic ways of communicating and working could then be examined not only as ethical commitments but as candidate mechanisms for reducing the prediction error that may underlie such affective responses. The present study does not directly test these social-perception questions, but by establishing whether unexpectedness shapes comfort in the non-social case, it lays groundwork for future research to examine whether the same mechanism operates in social perception and whether the normalisation of autistic traits offers a tractable route to alleviating the ostracisation that currently follows from their being perceived as oddities.
